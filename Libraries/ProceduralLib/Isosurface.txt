Isosurface plan
===============

ProceduralLib makes a CMesh from any shape that can say how far a point is from its surface: spheres, cylinders, cubes, convex
hulls, and what is made by joining and cutting them.  The mesh it makes is an ordinary CMesh, so a CMeshEditor, a CScene and both
renderers take it as they take any other.

Built: ShapePlan.txt's step 8, in the sub-steps under Steps, each with what it found.  What was decided and what was only proposed
is said at each point.


The user's decisions (2026-11-15)
---------------------------------
  The surface is extracted by dual contouring on an octree, with each cell's vertex placed by a fit to the planes at its crossings
  (Ju, Losasso, Schaefer and Warren 2002).  Plain marching cubes was recommended first and dropped: it puts vertices only on cell
  edges, so a crease (a V, a mitred elbow, a cube's edge) is cut off by a bevel up to a cell wide, a notch narrower than a cell can
  vanish, and finer cells only make the bevel narrower.
  Detail goes where the shape needs it: the octree splits a cell where its normals disagree or its corners don't predict its middle.
  Spheres, cylinders, cubes and convex hulls must all work, and so must what is made by joining and cutting them.
  Classic marching cubes is still built, as a baseline to compare against, step 8.3.


What a shape is
---------------
A shape is a CShape (SupportLib's ShapePlan.txt, the user's decision): a CObject whose Distance is negative inside, positive
outside and zero on its surface, with a Gradient.  Every shape extends it, and the isosurface takes a Ptr<CShape>.  What the
isosurface needs of it:
  Exactly zero is outside.  A cube whose faces lie on the grid's planes samples zeros on them, and taking zero both ways gives
  faces twice or not at all.  Every test that samples a sign uses the one function for it.
  Distance may be a bound and not exact: it must never be more than the true distance, as empty space is skipped by it.  min and
  max of exact distances are bounds, and so is a smooth union, so joining and cutting keep this.
  A shape that can give its gradient exactly should.  Central differences taken across a crease average its two faces, and the
  plane fit then can't find the crease.  Central differences are the fallback, with a step well under the smallest cell.
  The gradient needn't be a unit vector; the fit normalises it.

Shapes to start with, and what each tests:
  Sphere       Smooth, no creases.  The fit has no single answer and has to be steadied (see Placing a cell's vertex).
  Box          Faces, edges and corners: one, two and three planes.  Its faces on the grid's planes test the zero rule.
  Cylinder     A smooth side and two circular creases at its caps.  The side's normals only vary around its axis.
  Convex hull  The largest of its faces' signed plane distances (ShapePlan.txt's CConvexHull, from CQuickHull).  Exact inside and on the
               surface, a bound outside near edges and corners, which is allowed.  Its gradient is the winning face's normal,
               exactly the plane the fit wants, so a hull's faces come out flat and its edges sharp.
  Union, intersection, subtraction   min, max and max(a, -b).  Where two shapes meet there is a new crease, and each crossing
               takes its normal from whichever shape wins there, so a cylinder pushed into a box has a sharp seam.
  Smooth union                       Quilez's polynomial smooth min.  Its seam is rounded, by design.  A bound.
  Transform    A shape moved, turned and scaled evenly; the distance is scaled back.  An uneven scale gives a bound only if the
               distance is scaled back by the smallest scale (ShapePlan.txt, step 6).

A closed CMesh as a shape (closest triangle through a spatial index, inside by winding number or pseudo normals, Baerentzen and
Aanaes 2005) is left until something needs it.  It is far slower than the shapes above.  ShapePlan.txt has it as CMeshShape, which keeps its search structure in its own arrays.


Building the octree
-------------------
The octree covers a box given by the caller: its root cell, the smallest cell size and the largest.  Proposed:

  A cell is split while it is bigger than the largest size.
  A cell whose centre is further from the surface than half its diagonal has no surface in it, so it isn't split and makes no
  faces.  This is what a distance buys over an inside / outside test, and it is still safe when the distance is a bound.
  A cell that may hold the surface is split, down to the smallest size, if:
    the normals at its crossings differ by more than an angle (a crease, or a bend tighter than the cell), or
    the distance at its centre differs from what its corners' distances predict (trilinearly) by more than a tolerance, which
    finds a notch or spike thinner than the cell whose walls the corners don't see.
  Nothing thinner than the smallest cell is expected to survive.  A sphere smaller than it may vanish.

Cells that make no faces are dropped, so a scene mostly of empty space costs little.  Corner distances were to be shared between
neighbouring cells, keyed by their position on the finest grid, so each is worked out once.  They aren't yet: a distance is the
same each time it's worked out, so cells agree on their signs, and BaseLib's maps count with a size, which a big octree could
pass with a 16 bit one.


Placing a cell's vertex
-----------------------
Each edge of a leaf cell whose ends differ in sign has a crossing.  The crossing is found along the edge (proposed: a few steps of
the secant method on the distance, as linear interpolation alone puts it off a curved surface), and the shape's gradient there is
its normal.  The vertex is where the planes through the crossings, along their normals, best meet:

  minimise  sum over crossings  (n . (x - p))^2

Proposed, as Ju et al. and most implementations since do it:
  Accumulate the 3x3 matrix A'A, the vector A'b and b'b, which are small and add up, so cells can be merged later.
  Solve relative to the mass point (the crossings' average), with a pseudo inverse of A'A whose eigenvalues under a hundredth of
  the largest are taken as zero (A's singular values under a tenth: A'A's eigenvalues are their squares).  So a sphere's or a flat face's vertex is near the mass point instead of anywhere along the
  surface, a cylinder's side doesn't slide along its axis, and two or three planes still give their edge or corner exactly.
  If the vertex is still outside its cell it is clamped back in, and the mass point is used if that makes the error worse.
BaseLib has no eigen or singular value solver.  A 3x3 symmetric Jacobi rotation solver is small and is written for this, in
BaseLib, as it is plain arithmetic something else may want (the user's decision, 2026-11-15).


Making the faces
----------------
Each edge with a crossing is shared by four cells (around it), and their four vertices make one quad.  On an octree some of those
cells are bigger, and the edge is taken from the smallest of them, so a big cell's vertex is used by several quads and nothing
cracks between sizes.  The quad is wound so its face looks from negative to positive, anticlockwise from outside, as a scene
file's polygons are, and TestMeshShapes' checks (closed, facing out) say so.

The mesh has one corner per cell vertex, shared by every quad that uses it: one connected mesh, so CMesh's connectivity, the
modifiers and smoothing work on it.  A quad is added as two triangles split along the diagonal that follows the crease, as the
other diagonal zigzags a crease.  That isn't the split whose triangles' normals agree best: a quad across a box's edge has two of
its vertices on the edge, and splitting between them gives faces at a right angle, which is right, where the other split gives
two faces that agree better and cut through the box.  Proposed: the diagonal whose middle is nearer the surface, by the shape's
distance there.  CMesh::AddQuad picks the flattest split itself (IsMeshQuadCutFirstToThird), which is right for a general mesh
and not here, so the isosurface's quads are added as two AddFaces.

Normals are per face corner in a CMesh.  Proposed: flat face normals, then CMeshMergeNormals (SupportLib), which makes the
normals at a corner one average where they are within an angle of each other, so a sphere shades smooth and a box's edges stay
sharp.  Using the shape's gradient at each vertex instead is smoother on curves but blurs creases, and needs the angle test
anyway.  CMeshSmoothGroups' SGS_Angle does the same by smoothing groups, which a scene file keeps.  CMeshMergeNormals it is
(the user's decision, 2026-11-15), as it works per corner on any mesh.

UVs and materials aren't made yet.  See Open.


Known limits
------------
Dual contouring can make a surface that isn't a manifold (two sheets through one vertex) where one cell holds parts of the
surface that don't touch.  Manifold Dual Contouring (Schaefer, Ju and Warren 2007) splits such a cell's vertex (step 8.8).  It
does so where two sheets' normals are over about 100 degrees apart, so a pinch can still be left where they're nearer, or in an
octree leaf bigger than the smallest.
Marching cubes' pieces are only what the corners show: a box's edge just outside a cell leaves its two faces as two pieces, and a
cell can hold pieces of two faces whose edge passes outside it, where the vertex, clamped back in, is up to 0.3 of a cell off.
A corner can be cut off when the cell holding it doesn't cross all three of its faces.  Splitting by normal angle usually reaches
it, as the normals of a corner's faces differ by a right angle.
The vertex is a fit, so a surface may cross itself near a tight crease; the clamp keeps it within its cell.


Steps
-----
The shapes and their combinations are ShapePlan.txt's steps 1 to 7, in BaseLib and SupportLib with their own tests.  The rest is
that plan's step 8, done in these sub-steps, each with its tests in TestProceduralLib and committed when it passes:

8.1 TestProceduralLib builds and runs on Linux: Release x64, Debug 32-bit, Size16 and ASan.  Its own mesh checks, as it can't
    use TestSupportLib's: closed (every edge on two faces, corners less edges plus faces as expected), facing out (each face's
    normal agrees with the shape's gradient at its middle) and on the surface (every corner within a tolerance of it).  They're
    tried on SupportLib's editors' meshes with the shapes of the same values, and on a mesh turned inside out.
    Done: MeshMeasures in TestProceduralLib, whose CMake has the Size16 option now.
8.2 Sampling.  A grid over a box: the shape's distance at each corner, each worked out once, each edge's crossing found on the
    distance with exactly zero outside, and the shape's gradient there as the crossing's normal.  Marching cubes and dual
    contouring both use it.
    Done: CIsosurfaceGrid, the crossing found by the Illinois method (the secant through the bracket, the end that stays twice
    halved).  Its arrays are large ones, so a grid can pass 64KB with a 16 bit size.
8.3 Marching cubes, the baseline, with each vertex shared by the grid edge it's on.  Tests: a sphere, cylinder and torus come out
    closed, facing out and on the surface.  A box's edges come out bevelled, which is what 8.5 is measured against.
    Done: CMarchingCubes.  Its 256 cases are made, not Bourke's tables typed in: each face of the cube is walked anticlockwise
    seen from outside and a line joins each crossing into the inside to the next out of it.  A face's lines depend on its own
    corners, so neighbouring cubes agree and nothing cracks, and two inside corners across a face are kept apart.  A gyroid cut
    by a sphere (TestGyroid.h) meets every case.  Where a cut meets the surface at a sharp angle, a bevel's middle can be won by
    the other shape, so facing out is only checked on the gyroid's own faces there.
8.4 The plane fit: a cell's crossings and normals accumulated (A'A, A'b, b'b and the mass point) and solved relative to the mass
    point, BaseLib's 3x3 symmetric Jacobi solver giving the pseudo inverse, small singular values dropped, and the vertex
    clamped to its cell.  Tests: three planes give their corner exactly, two the nearest point on their edge, one the mass
    point, and the solver's own.
    Done: BaseLib's SFloat32Symmetric3x3 (float32, as BaseLib compiles for the 65C64 and W65C816) and SIsosurfaceFit, relative to
    the cell's centre.  Its cut off is a hundredth of A'A's largest eigenvalue: a tenth drops a face crossed at one or two of a
    cell's edges when another is crossed at many.
8.5 Dual contouring on a uniform grid: a vertex for each cell the surface crosses and a quad for each crossed edge, from the four
    cells around it, wound from inside to outside and split along the diagonal whose middle is nearer the surface, added as two
    AddFaces.  Flat normals, then CMeshMergeNormals.  Tests: a box's corners and edges exactly where they are, a cylinder's rims
    sharp, the seam of a cylinder pushed into a box sharp, a sphere's vertices on it, each closed and facing out.
    Done: CDualContouring.  At 0.25 cells, a turned box's corners are exact, its volume within 1% (marching cubes loses 4%) and its
    area right to 2 decimals; a cylinder has 80 corners on its rims (marching cubes 16) and its volume within 0.5%; the union's
    seam has 22 (marching cubes none).  Not every vertex is on a crease: a cell can hold pieces of two faces whose edge passes
    outside it, and the fit, clamped back into the cell or the mass point, is up to 0.3 of a cell off.  Letting the vertex out of
    its cell by even a tenth of one folds faces over instead, so it stays clamped, and the octree's splitting is to shrink such
    cells.  A gyroid has a few faces folded (4 of 7000) where a cell holds two pieces of surface (Known limits).
    CMeshMergeNormals' arrays are large ones now: a thousand faces passed 64KB with a 16 bit size.
8.6 The octree: a cell skipped when its distance shows the surface can't reach it, and split when its normals differ by more than
    an angle or its centre's distance differs from what its corners predict.  An edge is taken from the smallest cell around it,
    so nothing cracks between sizes.  Tests: closed with no cracks, a V notch thinner than the largest cell kept, a flat face
    left coarse, and far fewer cells than the uniform grid.
    Done: CIsosurfaceOctree.  Faces are made on the minimal edges (each leaf's edges with no smaller leaf around them), and each
    crossing is added to the fits of all the leaves around its edge, so a big leaf beside small ones has a vertex.  Two things
    the plan didn't have, both against faces folding over where sizes change: the tree is balanced (leaves beside each other
    differ by one size at most), and a crossing counts in a leaf's fit by its edge's length over the leaf's size, so a big
    leaf's small neighbours don't pull its vertex over theirs.  A turned box went from 43 faces facing in to 1.  Splitting by
    the distance needs a bound, so the test gyroid is divided by its steepest slope.  At cells from 1 to 0.0625, a sphere has a
    quarter of a uniform grid's corners and a box half, a V notch a third of the largest cell is kept, and a gyroid cut by a
    sphere is closed with none of its own faces folded.  A box's corners are within a quarter of the smallest cell, as the
    cell holding one may not cross all three of its faces (Known limits).
8.7 Shape to mesh: one CMeshSourceModifier in ProceduralLib holding a Ptr<CShape>, now that modifiers are CObjects
    (ScenePlan.txt, step 2).  A primitive is made exactly by its own mesh editor with the same values (ShapePlan.txt, section
    5); anything else goes through the isosurface.  Tests: a primitive's mesh is its editor's, a combination's is closed and
    facing out, and a small scene of shapes drawn with CRasterFrame matches saved bytes.
    Done: CShape::AddMesh in SupportLib (the sphere, box, cylinder, cone, tube and torus) and CMeshShapeEditor in ProceduralLib.
    It makes faces only.  CMeshFlatFaces and CMeshMergeNormals after it gave normals at first, but merging at 30 degrees split
    corners where coarse faces fanned through more (4 of 5300 on five spheres smoothly joined, which showed as creases in
    WindowInputTester's isosurface tab).  CMeshShapeNormals gives them from the shape: the gradient at each corner, shared,
    where its faces' sides agree within 45 degrees, and each face's own side's at a crease.  The scene is matched within 8 a channel in
    all but a hundredth of its pixels, not byte for byte: g++ contracts a * b + c into FMA instructions when it optimises
    (-march=haswell, and its rule against that is for C only), so Release and Debug builds differ in floats' last bits, even
    SupportLib's sphere editor's, and that moved 32 pixels by a level or two.  It's left out of Size16 builds, as RenderLib's
    cooked mesh lists count with a size.  A union's two slivers at a cylinder's rim, a thousandth apart, can face either way.
8.8 Manifold dual contouring, only if 8.6's tests find sheets meeting at a vertex.
    Done, as they did: a gyroid had 9 corners pinched uniformly and 12 on the octree, now 0 and 1.  A cell's sheets are marching
    cubes' pieces, its ambiguous faces decided by the asymptotic decider (Nielson and Hamann 1991), joined where their normals
    are under about 100 degrees apart: without that a box's edge just outside a cell dented it.  The octree splits its smallest
    leaves' vertices; bigger ones keep one.
Classic marching cubes' ambiguous cases (MC33) are dropped, as marching cubes is only the baseline.


Open
----
float32 or float64 inside the fit.  The fit subtracts nearly equal numbers on a far away cell; positions relative to the cell's
centre may be enough.
UVs.  An isosurface has none.  A box projection (each face takes the UVs of the plane its normal is nearest) is the simplest;
triplanar blending needs three samples and isn't something a CMesh can hold.
Materials.  A distance query could also say which shape won, so each face gets its shape's material slot.
Coordinates.  ScenePlan.txt's open question applies: a scene file is right handed with y up.
Threads.  Cells are independent once their corners are known, so the octree's leaves could be done on CJobs.
Level of detail between blocks of an octree made separately (Transvoxel, Lengyel 2010) waits for something big enough to need it.
