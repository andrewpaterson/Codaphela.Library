Isosurface plan
===============

ProceduralLib makes a CMesh from any shape that can say how far a point is from its surface: spheres, cylinders, cubes, convex
hulls, and what is made by joining and cutting them.  The mesh it makes is an ordinary CMesh, so a CMeshEditor, a CScene and both
renderers take it as they take any other.

Nothing here is built yet.  What is decided and what is only proposed is said at each point.


The user's decisions (2026-11-15)
---------------------------------
  The surface is extracted by dual contouring on an octree, with each cell's vertex placed by a fit to the planes at its crossings
  (Ju, Losasso, Schaefer and Warren 2002).  Plain marching cubes was recommended first and dropped: it puts vertices only on cell
  edges, so a crease (a V, a mitred elbow, a cube's edge) is cut off by a bevel up to a cell wide, a notch narrower than a cell can
  vanish, and finer cells only make the bevel narrower.
  Detail goes where the shape needs it: the octree splits a cell where its normals disagree or its corners don't predict its middle.
  Spheres, cylinders, cubes and convex hulls must all work, and so must what is made by joining and cutting them.
  Classic marching cubes is still built, as a baseline to compare against, step 3.


What a shape is
---------------
A shape is a CShape (SupportLib's ShapePlan.txt, the user's decision): a CObject whose Distance is negative inside, positive
outside and zero on its surface, with a Gradient.  Every shape extends it, and the isosurface takes a Ptr<CShape>.  What the
isosurface needs of it:
  Exactly zero is outside.  A cube whose faces lie on the grid's planes samples zeros on them, and taking zero both ways gives
  faces twice or not at all.  Every test that samples a sign uses the one function for it.
  Distance may be a bound and not exact: it must never be more than the true distance, as empty space is skipped by it.  min and
  max of exact distances are bounds, and so is a smooth union, so joining and cutting keep this.
  A shape that can give its gradient exactly should.  Central differences taken across a crease average its two faces, and the
  plane fit then can't find the crease.  Central differences are the fallback, with a step well under the smallest cell.
  The gradient needn't be a unit vector; the fit normalises it.

Shapes to start with, and what each tests:
  Sphere       Smooth, no creases.  The fit has no single answer and has to be steadied (see Placing a cell's vertex).
  Box          Faces, edges and corners: one, two and three planes.  Its faces on the grid's planes test the zero rule.
  Cylinder     A smooth side and two circular creases at its caps.  The side's normals only vary around its axis.
  Convex hull  The largest of its faces' signed plane distances (ShapePlan.txt's CConvexHull, from CQuickHull).  Exact inside and on the
               surface, a bound outside near edges and corners, which is allowed.  Its gradient is the winning face's normal,
               exactly the plane the fit wants, so a hull's faces come out flat and its edges sharp.
  Union, intersection, subtraction   min, max and max(a, -b).  Where two shapes meet there is a new crease, and each crossing
               takes its normal from whichever shape wins there, so a cylinder pushed into a box has a sharp seam.
  Smooth union                       Quilez's polynomial smooth min.  Its seam is rounded, by design.  A bound.
  Transform    A shape moved, turned and scaled evenly; the distance is scaled back.  An uneven scale gives a bound only if the
               distance is divided by the largest scale.

A closed CMesh as a shape (closest triangle through a spatial index, inside by winding number or pseudo normals, Baerentzen and
Aanaes 2005) is left until something needs it.  It is far slower than the shapes above.  ShapePlan.txt has it as CMeshShape, which keeps its search structure in its own arrays.


Building the octree
-------------------
The octree covers a box given by the caller: its root cell, the smallest cell size and the largest.  Proposed:

  A cell is split while it is bigger than the largest size.
  A cell whose centre is further from the surface than half its diagonal has no surface in it, so it isn't split and makes no
  faces.  This is what a distance buys over an inside / outside test, and it is still safe when the distance is a bound.
  A cell that may hold the surface is split, down to the smallest size, if:
    the normals at its crossings differ by more than an angle (a crease, or a bend tighter than the cell), or
    the distance at its centre differs from what its corners' distances predict (trilinearly) by more than a tolerance, which
    finds a notch or spike thinner than the cell whose walls the corners don't see.
  Nothing thinner than the smallest cell is expected to survive.  A sphere smaller than it may vanish.

Cells that make no faces are dropped, so a scene mostly of empty space costs little.  Corner distances are shared between
neighbouring cells, keyed by their position on the finest grid, so each is worked out once.


Placing a cell's vertex
-----------------------
Each edge of a leaf cell whose ends differ in sign has a crossing.  The crossing is found along the edge (proposed: a few steps of
the secant method on the distance, as linear interpolation alone puts it off a curved surface), and the shape's gradient there is
its normal.  The vertex is where the planes through the crossings, along their normals, best meet:

  minimise  sum over crossings  (n . (x - p))^2

Proposed, as Ju et al. and most implementations since do it:
  Accumulate the 3x3 matrix A'A, the vector A'b and b'b, which are small and add up, so cells can be merged later.
  Solve relative to the mass point (the crossings' average), with a pseudo inverse of A'A whose singular values under a tenth of
  the largest are taken as zero.  So a sphere's or a flat face's vertex is near the mass point instead of anywhere along the
  surface, a cylinder's side doesn't slide along its axis, and two or three planes still give their edge or corner exactly.
  If the vertex is still outside its cell it is clamped back in, and the mass point is used if that makes the error worse.
BaseLib has no eigen or singular value solver.  A 3x3 symmetric Jacobi rotation solver is small and is written for this,
in ProceduralLib unless something else wants it.


Making the faces
----------------
Each edge with a crossing is shared by four cells (around it), and their four vertices make one quad.  On an octree some of those
cells are bigger, and the edge is taken from the smallest of them, so a big cell's vertex is used by several quads and nothing
cracks between sizes.  The quad is wound so its face looks from negative to positive, anticlockwise from outside, as a scene
file's polygons are, and TestMeshShapes' checks (closed, facing out) say so.

The mesh has one corner per cell vertex, shared by every quad that uses it: one connected mesh, so CMesh's connectivity, the
modifiers and smoothing work on it.  A quad is added as two triangles split along the diagonal that follows the crease, as the
other diagonal zigzags a crease.  That isn't the split whose triangles' normals agree best: a quad across a box's edge has two of
its vertices on the edge, and splitting between them gives faces at a right angle, which is right, where the other split gives
two faces that agree better and cut through the box.  Proposed: the diagonal whose middle is nearer the surface, by the shape's
distance there.  CMesh::AddQuad picks the flattest split itself (IsMeshQuadCutFirstToThird), which is right for a general mesh
and not here, so the isosurface's quads are added as two AddFaces.

Normals are per face corner in a CMesh.  Proposed: flat face normals, then CMeshMergeNormals (SupportLib), which makes the
normals at a corner one average where they are within an angle of each other, so a sphere shades smooth and a box's edges stay
sharp.  Using the shape's gradient at each vertex instead is smoother on curves but blurs creases, and needs the angle test
anyway.  CMeshSmoothGroups' SGS_Angle does the same by smoothing groups, which a scene file keeps; either will do, and which is
for step 6.

UVs and materials aren't made yet.  See Open.


Known limits
------------
Dual contouring can make a surface that isn't a manifold (two sheets through one vertex) where one cell holds parts of the
surface that don't touch.  Manifold Dual Contouring (Schaefer, Ju and Warren 2007) splits such a cell's vertex.  Whether it is
needed is found by testing, step 7.
A corner can be cut off when the cell holding it doesn't cross all three of its faces.  Splitting by normal angle usually reaches
it, as the normals of a corner's faces differ by a right angle.
The vertex is a fit, so a surface may cross itself near a tight crease; the clamp keeps it within its cell.


Steps
-----
Each step has its tests in TestProceduralLib.

1. and 2. The shapes and their combinations are ShapePlan.txt's steps 1 to 7, in BaseLib and SupportLib with their own tests.
   This plan's steps 3 to 7 are that plan's step 8, with the modifier that meshes a primitive by its own mesh editor.
3. Marching cubes on a uniform grid, the baseline: Bourke's 256 case tables (from Bloyd), vertices shared by the grid edge they
   are on.  Tests: TestMeshShapes' closed and facing out checks on each shape, and every vertex within a tolerance of the surface.
4. Dual contouring on a uniform grid: crossings, the plane fit with its Jacobi solver, quads split along the crease.  Tests: a
   box's corners and edges exactly where they are (marching cubes' aren't), a sphere's vertices on it, closed and facing out.
5. The octree: empty cells skipped by distance, cells split by normal angle and by the centre test, faces between cells of
   different sizes.  Tests: closed with no cracks, a V notch thinner than the largest cell kept, a flat face left coarse.
6. A CMeshSourceModifier that runs it (proposed CMeshIsosurfaceEditor in ProceduralLib), so it sits on a CMeshEditor's stack, and
   a CScene of shapes drawn with CRasterFrame.  Tests: a frame compared with saved bytes.
7. Manifold dual contouring, if step 5's tests find sheets meeting at a vertex.
8. Classic marching cubes' ambiguous cases (MC33, Lewiner 2003), only if step 3 is kept and shows holes.


Open
----
float32 or float64 inside the fit.  The fit subtracts nearly equal numbers on a far away cell; positions relative to the cell's
centre may be enough.
UVs.  An isosurface has none.  A box projection (each face takes the UVs of the plane its normal is nearest) is the simplest;
triplanar blending needs three samples and isn't something a CMesh can hold.
Materials.  A distance query could also say which shape won, so each face gets its shape's material slot.
Coordinates.  ScenePlan.txt's open question applies: a scene file is right handed with y up.
Threads.  Cells are independent once their corners are known, so the octree's leaves could be done on CJobs.
Level of detail between blocks of an octree made separately (Transvoxel, Lengyel 2010) waits for something big enough to need it.
