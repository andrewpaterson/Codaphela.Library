Scene plan
==========

CScene, CInstance, CConnection, CConnectionAnimation, CCamera and CLight describe a scene, in floats.  A scene file fills them and
they write one, and nothing else uses them yet.  A renderer draws what is converted from them, one way, from the description to what
is drawn.  RasterLib is the renderer today and a Vulkan one is to come, so nothing here is only for RasterLib unless it says so.


What the objects are
--------------------
  CScene                    Instances, lights, cameras, animations and particles.  Everything else is reached through what uses it.
    CInstance               One thing in the game, one character say.
      Ptr<CSkeleton>        Shared with other instances, from step 8.  Its bones, each a CBone.
      CArray<CConnection>   Its own, in the order a file holds them.  One for each bone, which points at its CBone.
      CArray<CInstanceMesh>
        Ptr<CMeshEditor>    Shared with other instances.  Holds the mesh and its modifier stack.
        CArray<CConnection> The instance's connections the mesh's matrices use.  One for a rigid mesh, one per matrix for a
                            skinned one.
        Materials           In place of the mesh's defaults.
    CLight, CCamera         Not instanced.  Each has its own Ptr<CConnection>.

Two characters are two CInstances that share their meshes and bones and are drawn in different places.

A CConnection is something spatial that lights, cameras, meshes or other game information can be attached to.

  Kind        What                                 Reaches its connection by
  Shared      CMeshEditor, bones                   The instance's CInstanceMesh, which holds the connections it uses
  Not shared  CLight, CCamera, small game objects  Its own Ptr<CConnection>

Shared data can't point at a connection, as each instance has its own, so what an instance uses of it is in an object of the
instance's: a CInstanceMesh holds the editor, shared, and the instance's connections its matrices use, which a file holds as
their places in the instance's list.  A skinned mesh's SSkinWeight::iMatrix numbers its own matrices, not the instance's
connections, which is why the connections are per mesh.

Lights and cameras are too small to be worth sharing and aren't instanced.  Two lanterns are two CLights.  A light on its own
has its own connection, and one carried by a character has a connection whose parent is the character's hand.

Pose (skinning, morphs) is per instance and never changes the shared CMesh, as two characters share it and stand differently.

CMeshEditor holds the mesh and the modifier stack and produces the final mesh to be cooked.


From the scene to what is drawn
-------------------------------
  CMeshEditor::GetOutput --> CMesh                                  Runs the stack.
    1. Cook: when it has changed.                                   SRenderMeshData and friends.  Plain, for any renderer.
    2. Create: per device.                                          CRasterRenderMesh or CVulkanRenderMesh, from CRenderMesh.
    3. Extract: every frame, from connections, camera and lights.   SRenderFrame
    4. Draw.                                                        CRasteriser, or Vulkan command buffers.

  Source                 Cooked or extracted             Raster                     Vulkan
  CMesh                  Unique vertices, submeshes by   CRasterMesh                Vertex and index buffers, ranges
                         material slot, opaque and
                         transparent, bounds
  CMaterial              Parameters, image references    Flat colour at most        Descriptor set, pipeline key
  CImage                 Pixels in a format, with mips   Ignored for now            VkImage, view, sampler
  CMeshSkin              Weights, inverse matrices       Skinned on the CPU         Vertex attributes, matrix palette
  CConnection            World matrix, parity            asAxes and sOffset         Per instance buffer, front face
  CCamera                View and projection             CRasterCamera              Uniform buffer
  CLight                 A list of lights                CRasteriser's light values Uniform buffer
  CConnectionAnimation   (changes CConnection)           Never sees it              Never sees it

A cooked mesh keeps a table from the CMesh's corners to its vertices, while it is being edited, for updating it in place.
Position and normal are a stream of their own, so that a change of shape or pose only touches them.  CRasterMesh already
splits this way, with masVertices apart from its triangles.

The integer scale, quantising and turn-only axes are the raster renderer's own, and so is what it does with a mirrored matrix.


When something changes
----------------------
Each frame CRenderScene walks the scene and looks at the editors' change counters.  Nothing has to notify it.

  Change                                   What happens
  Structure, or any edit under modifiers   The stack runs again and a new render mesh replaces the old at a frame boundary.
  Shape, with no modifiers                 The same at first.  Updated in place once something needs that speed.
  Pose                                     Per instance.  The shared CMesh and the stack aren't touched.
  A mesh no longer used                    Its Ptr is dropped and the graph frees it.

Modifiers may add or remove vertices, so a mesh with any modifiers can't be updated in place: there may be no old vertex to
overwrite.  A heavy stack is too slow to run on every mouse move, so an editor may run it when a drag ends, or draw the
unmodified mesh during the drag.  That is the editor's choice.

The graph can free a render mesh at any moment, while the GPU may still be using it.  On Vulkan its Free only queues its buffers
until that frame's fence signals.  Raster frees them at once.

Destruction order is what CComponent does with Nes(M_Pointer(mpWindow)).  A render mesh holds a Nes pointer to its CRenderer, so
every mesh is freed before the renderer, and the pointer is still valid in the mesh's Free.  The renderer's lookup pointing back
at its meshes is fine: only a cycle made entirely of Nes pointers can't be ordered (see TestObjectFreeOrdering).  The renderer's
Free waits for the device, empties the queue and destroys the device.  The factory isn't an object in the graph and is made by the
app, so it outlives them all, as CNativeWindowFactory does CWindow.  A mesh's Free shouldn't read a collection on the renderer:
after an explicit Kill only pointer fields are put back for it, not collection entries.


The rule
--------
Nothing in the scene knows a renderer exists, and a renderer only sees what it is given.

  SupportLib        The scene.  Knows nothing of renderers.
  RenderLib         Cooking, CRenderer (per device, holds the shared resources), CRenderScene, the abstract CRenderMesh and
                    friends, and the abstract CRenderFactory.  The app makes the factory, as it does CNativeWindowFactory.
  RasterRenderLib   Implements the factory using RasterLib.  Converts floats and scene types into what RasterLib wants.
  VulkanLib         Implements the factory using Vulkan.
  RasterLib         A standalone fixed point renderer.  Takes integers and draws, and is usable with no scene at all.  Standalone
                    means no SupportLib or RenderLib.  BaseLib and StandardLib are part of it.

These stay out of RasterLib:
  Transforms with parents        CConnection's local and world matrices.
  Lights as objects              CLight.  The rasteriser is given plain values to shade with.
  Keyframes or time              CConnectionAnimation.
  A list of what's in the world  CScene.

Drawing features in fixed point go in RasterLib, and whatever touches floats or scene types goes in RasterRenderLib, which is a
library of its own so that RasterLib depends on neither RenderLib nor the scene.  Since step 4 RasterLib includes nothing
from SupportLib: RasterRenderLib's BuildRasterMesh makes a CRasterMesh from a CMesh.  CRasterCamera keeps its rotation in a
float matrix, which it converts to fixed point once a frame.

A CScene never creates a CRasterMesh.  RasterRenderLib does.

CRasterMesh is a CObject, so that anything can share one through a Ptr, with or without a scene.  SRasterInstance stays a plain
struct with a raw pointer, as it is per-frame data used by the worker threads.  Whatever builds the list of instances holds the
Ptr<CRasterMesh>s, which keeps them alive during the draw.

Each renderer subclasses CRenderMesh directly.  CCanvas has a native object beneath it because it has a lot of logic that doesn't
depend on the host.  A render mesh has little, so that layer would buy almost nothing, and a native object couldn't hold a Ptr.


The scene file format
---------------------
The goal behind this plan is a file that meshes and whole scenes can be saved to.  It comes before the steps below and needs none
of them.  It is simpler than the CObject graph, so that something like a 3ds Max exporter can write it with no object system: the
old exporter filled engine objects and called their Save, so the format was whatever those classes were at the time.

CChunkFile is the main format.  OBJ is kept for plain meshes, and glTF import may come later for what other tools make.  None of the
open formats fits as the main one: OBJ has no skin, animation or hierarchy, glTF has no polygons, smoothing groups or Blinn
materials, FBX is closed, and COLLADA and USD are large.

  Reader and writer   Calls, not a model in memory.  cWriter.BeginMesh, WritePositions, WriteFaces, EndMesh and so on.  In SupportLib, as
                      they're only used with CMesh and co, though they only use BaseLib, and an exporter never sees chunk names or
                      layouts.  What fills a CMesh or a CScene from the reader lives beside them.
  Extension           .DRG, with a version header.  CChunkFile's user ID says which kind of .DRG it is, as ChunkFileObjectWriter's
                      does, and a required chunk holds the format's version.  A reader refuses a version it doesn't know.
  Modifier stacks     Not in this format.  It holds final meshes, as a Max scene arrives with its modifiers applied.  Editable
                      stacks are for internal saving through the CObject graph, later, if an editor needs it.

The rules that keep it from going the way of the old files:
  Each attribute is a chunk of its own: Positions, Normals, UVs.  A new attribute is a new chunk, so nothing changes size.  Readers
  ask for chunks by name, so an old file lacks a new chunk and gets a default, and an old reader ignores a new one.  The version
  only changes when that isn't enough.
  No struct is written whole.  Only fixed size types: no size, and no pointers.
  References are indices into lists in the same file.  The writer checks them when the file closes.
  Every result is checked.  A failed write fails the export.  (Melee.DRG's mesh faces were lost because a Save's result was ignored.)
  CChunkFile's own layout must stay put, so a saved sample file and a test fail if it changes.

Format steps:
A. Fix SupportLib's OBJWriter, see below.  Done.
B. The reader and writer, holding what Goober and Melee contain: meshes, polygons, smoothing groups, UVs, skin, materials, images,
   connections, animation, instances, lights, cameras and particles.  Done: CSceneFileWriter and CSceneFileReader in SupportLib, with the format described in
   SceneFileGeneral.h.  Faces are polygons, a triangle being a polygon of 3.  A failure sticks, and Close then fails and leaves no
   file.  Input/Scene/Sample.DRG in TestSupportLib uses all of it and fails its test if the bytes change.
C. Convert the old files, see below.  Done: OldDragonConverter in OldDragonLib, run by Tools\ConvertOldDragon, whose ConvertAll.py
   lists what came from where.
D. Render the converted files, including Goober's walk, as value checks can't catch a wrong rule.  Done: Tools\RenderSceneFile draws
   them with RasterLib and goes once CScene can do this (step E and on).  What it found is below.
E. Fill a CMesh and then a CScene from the reader.  Write them too.  Done: CScene::ReadFile and WriteFile, with SceneFileMesh for a mesh in
   an editor.  This needed step 1 below.  All the converted files go through a CScene and then render to the same bytes as before.
F. A Max exporter writing through the writer, if one is wanted.  It links SupportLib, StandardLib and BaseLib for that.

OBJ is done (step A): SaveOBJ and the reader handle positions, normals and one layer of UVs in both directions, and TestMeshShapes and
TestMeshOBJReader run again.  What OBJ still doesn't carry here:
  Reading takes polygons of any size and cuts them into faces, with the edges inside hidden.  Writing a CMesh gives triangles, as
  it has no polygons, and writing a CMeshEditor gives each polygon as one line where that can say what its triangles do: one loop
  with no corners inside, whose triangles agree on each corner's UV and normal.  When polygons move to CMesh (step 2) the one
  function does both.
  Holes aren't read, as OBJ has none, and a polygon that crosses itself is cut up from its first corner.
  It writes no smoothing groups, materials or object names, and reading ignores them.  Normals say how a mesh is smoothed.
  A file's normals must come before its faces.
  Positions are rounded to 1 / 65536 when written (BINARY_PRECISION, SFloat32Vec3::Fix).  That is about 0.015mm for a mesh in metres, as
  KiCad's newer exports are, so OBJ isn't a lossless format for those.  The chunk format writes floats as they are.
  The heading holds ENGINE_VERSION, so changing the version means changing the expected .OBJ files.

OldDragonLib is only used once, to convert the old files.  It stays in the repo, out of the regular builds and tests, in case a
conversion mistake turns up.  The new format owes nothing to the old layouts, and quirks are fixed in the converter and never reach
a new file: Melee's faces from Melee.max, diffuse red and opacity in their right places, and key times in what the new format defines.
A material's colours are the DRG's, which are white when it has a diffuse texture.  They only differ from the .max in Chenjesu Rim
(ambient red 0.30 in the DRG, 0.60 in the .max) and 04 - Default.

What the converter found the old files really do, which is in OldDragonConverter.h:
  An instance's and a sequence's connection numbers aren't places in the list of connections in Goober, as its 18 IK chains and finger
  helpers are in the list but aren't counted, so most point at the wrong one.  Instances and sequences agree with each other, so
  skinning from them looks right.  A sequence's name is what says which connection it moves, in every file.
  Pixels are BGR and BGRA in Into Oblivion and the 2009 files, but RGB and RGBA in the Max exporter's.  All have the top row first.
  Every sequence's last key is the one before it again, with no time.
  Into Oblivion's link nodes say what they place, and one link can place many things.  Its instances don't say.

What rendering them found, which is in SceneFileGeneral.h and OldDragonConverter.h:
  A scene is right handed with y up: the exporter's (x, z, -y) doesn't mirror, Into Oblivion projects with PerspectiveFovRH, and Goober
  faces +z with its left hand at +x.  A polygon goes anticlockwise, and a v of 0 is an image's top row, in all of them.
  A camera didn't mean the same in any two.  The exporter's look along their -y with -z up, so Melee's and Test.DRG's identity matrices
  look straight down.  Into Oblivion's one holds forward, up and left in its rows, which mirrors it, and its game never used it:
  Level.cpp puts a view of its own in that matrix when a level starts.  They now all look along -z with y up.
  Lights shine along their -y in the old files (Into Oblivion's Light.cpp takes -_21) and along -z in the new ones.  Nothing draws with
  them yet, so this rests on that code.
  A field of view is horizontal: 45 degrees in Melee and Test.DRG, and Into Oblivion's 0.589 is that times 3/4 (CameraHelper::Set).
  Arilou Headlights winds the wrong way, the only mesh that does.  It's small, and it's left as it is.
  Melee's one channel images aren't the alpha of their PNGs.  Nothing has drawn with them, so what they should do is still open.

What going through a CScene does to a file.  None of this changes how anything looks:
  What nothing uses isn't kept, as a CScene reaches things through what uses them.  Test.DRG loses all 19 of its materials, as its meshes have
  no slots.  Melee loses 21 materials and 2 images, PowerUps 2 materials, LevelOne a material and an image, and Goober its goober_ connection.
  The source files keep them if one turns out to matter.
  Normals made from smoothing groups are written, as well as the groups, so a re-saved Goober has a Normals chunk.  It's about the same size.
  Every material, connection and so on gets what a file left out written with its default.
  A polygon of more than one face starts at its lowest corner.  A skin's weights are written largest first.
  After that a file stays the same, byte for byte, however many times it goes through.

How the converted files were checked, once, as the converter only runs once:
  A decoder with no C++ in it found all 18 follow the format's rules, with Goober's 6134 triangles and Melee's 132,356.
  Goober's bones are all bones, each track has its own connection, its first key is its connection's world matrix, and its skin holds
  together at every key tried (edges at 0.996 to 1.033 of their rest length).  Its keys were world matrices then; since step 7 they're
  local, and its first key is its connection's local matrix.
  Images with a source were compared with it: Goober's 2 TGAs and Melee's PNGs.  Into Oblivion's BMPs are gone, so its order comes from
  its CTexture, which puts A8R8G8B8 ints straight into a locked Direct3D texture.  Rendering still has to show its V agrees.
  Melee's Mycon Spore Outer is 515x512 in the DRG and 512x512 as a PNG.  It's the DRG's that was converted, and it isn't sheared.

  Old file                                                 Goes to
  IOX2021\data\meshes, 15 DRGs                             IntoOblivionX
  StarControl1\Melee.DRG, with MaxFiles\Melee.max          StarControlRedux
  Star Control\Builds\StarControl\Test.DRG                 StarControlRedux
  Builds\ViewerV7beta2009.9.20\Goober.DRG, with Stars.csv  CommonLibraries, as a test scene

The old files are kept as they are.  IntoOblivionX and StarControlRedux move to the new CObjects model eventually; holding their
converted files is the start.  After this the new files are what's true, and everything reads them.


Steps
-----
These are the scene objects and the renderer.  Each is done when something needs it, with tests.  Skinning must be proved before
Vulkan.

1. Scene objects.  Done.  CScene is a CObject with its lists, and CFakeTracker and TrackerTypes are gone.  CInstance, CInstanceMesh, CLight and
   CCamera hold Ptrs to what they use, a mesh's faces hold slots (-1 for no material) in a list of Ptrs, and a CMaterialImage holds
   its Ptr<CImage> and how it's sampled.  Names are CChars and not object names, as many things in a file share one.
2. CMeshEditor.  Done.
   The editor's stack has its mesh as its source, GetOutput is the mesh with its modifiers applied (the mesh itself with none),
   and structure and shape counters say what changed.  ClearOutput lets the output go after cooking.  Modifiers only Modify, and
   Collapse makes the output the editor's mesh, keeping the same mesh.  A shape is a modifier, and is collapsed to edit its faces.
   Polygons and smoothing groups are opt-in details of CMesh, as they describe the shape.  Being opt-in keeps big meshes from
   generating connectivity for every face they add: an editor turns polygons on, and its AddFace builds them from its edge flags.
   A copy of a mesh keeps everything, so a modifier that only changes normals works on any mesh, and a shape keeps what the mesh
   it's added to has on that mesh's own faces.  Edge visibility and selections stay on the editor: they are what the user sees
   and does.  An edge is hidden when the faces on both sides are in one polygon (CMeshPolygons::GetFaceEdges).
   Left: CMesh::MovePosition, MovePositions, RemoveFace, RemoveFaces and RemoveCorner are empty, so moving and removing do
   nothing yet, and it isn't decided whether a move is to a position or by an offset.  SaveOBJ of a CMesh writes triangles even
   when it has polygons, as a test reading triangles back expects that.
3. CConnection updates.  Done: UpdateWorldMatrix makes its world matrix its local one times its parent's, and its local one with no
   parent, parents first, and sets its parity from it.  CScene::UpdateConnections does all of a scene's.  Nothing calls it on its own
   yet.  The converted files have their parents back (see Test scenes), so updating them leaves everything where it was.
4. RenderLib and RasterRenderLib: create and replace only.  Done.
   CRasterMesh::Build moved unchanged to RasterRenderLib's BuildRasterMesh, so RasterLib no longer includes SupportLib, and
   ColourARGB32.h stays in SupportLib as most of it and WindowLib use it.  CRasterMesh is a CObject: BoardScene holds its
   models in a CArray, VRMLCanvasDraw through a Ptr, and TestRasterLib's are Ptrs.  Its Save and Load are WriteFile and
   ReadFile, as they'd hide CObject's.
   RenderLib has the abstract CRenderFactory, CRenderer and CRenderMesh, and CRenderScene, whose Update makes a render mesh
   for each editor a scene's instances use, one for an editor many share, and a new one when the editor's structure, shape or
   modifier counter moves.  A mesh no longer used is let go.  The modifier counter is new: adding or touching a modifier
   changed the output without moving the other two.  RasterRenderLib's CRasterRenderFactory makes a CRasterRenderer, which
   has the one scene-wide scale, and CRasterRenderMeshes built where their mesh is at that scale.  The tests are in
   TestRasterLib.  Step 5's CRasterFrame is the first to call CRenderScene.
5. Extract.  Done: RasterRenderLib's CRasterFrame, whose Extract updates the render scene and makes what CRasteriser draws.
   Connections become SRasterInstances, one for each rigid mesh of each instance, at the renderer's scale.  A skinned mesh is
   posed since step 9.  The user's decisions:
     An instance's axes may scale and mirror.  Its light axes are their rotation alone, which the light is turned into the mesh
     by, and a mirrored instance's triangles are wound the other way.  The shade is clamped at 256, so it can't carry into the
     next colour channel.  An uneven scale shades as if the normals were only turned, which is very nearly right for the small
     scales the converted files have.
     The camera is the scene camera, and the viewer's controls write back to it: when CRasterCamera's change counter moves the
     next Extract puts the view into the camera's connection, and when the scene camera moves the camera follows it, keeping
     its distance to the point it turns around.  ECameraType decides the projection, and FOV across the target's width makes
     the focal length.
     CRasteriser shades with CRasterLights, which start as the light it always had.  Ambient is a light (LT_Ambient), and
     ambient lights add up.  Directional lights, one or two, add up too.  Point lights (omni, and spot without its cone) reach
     as far as they'd add a shade, or their range, worked out when they're added.  One is only worked out for an instance its
     reach gets to the bounds of, a bit each in a mask, and then only for a vertex within its reach.  All grey for now.
   What the rasteriser drew before is byte-identical: 36 golden frames over every draw path hashed the same after each change.
6. Move BoardScene onto a CScene.  Done, though the Board Configuration dialog is still to be run by hand.  The scene is in
   millimetres, one size for every mesh: a model is scaled to them with CMeshEditor::Scale as it's read, and an editor has no
   scale of its own beyond what a connection's matrix does.  The mainboard is an instance whose connections are its own and,
   as their children, one for each socket at the middle of the floor of its slot.  A board standing in a socket is an instance
   whose connection is a child of the socket's.  Each model is an editor that every socket holding that board shares.  The
   yaw stays the raster camera's SetOrbit, after Fit and FitOrbit once, and the frame writes it back to the scene camera's
   connection.  The user's decision: its pixels needn't stay byte-identical, as the models are now simplified in millimetres.
   CMeshRemoveDegenerate is on the models' stack, after CMeshEdgeCollapse, and BuildRasterMesh leaves out what its rounding
   leaves with no area.  The VRML read is lazy as it was: each model's mesh is saved after its modifiers, in millimetres, as a
   .DRG in Output/Models, which a cached model is read from instead.
7. CConnectionAnimation.  Done: keyframes change connections, and step 5 runs again.  A track (CConnectionAnimation) holds a Ptr to
   the connection it moves and its keys (SConnectionKey: a time and a matrix), and a CSceneAnimation groups tracks, which is what a
   file holds.  CAnimationPlayer plays one: its time, speed and whether it loops; Apply sets the connections' local matrices and
   CScene::UpdateConnections makes their world ones.  A player is the app's, not the file's.  The user's decisions:
     A key is the connection's local matrix, relative to its parent's, so a parent carries what's animated: Goober walks on the
     spot and its instance's connection moves it.  The format's rule changed from world matrices; the converter makes each old
     world key relative to its parent's world matrix at the same time, and of the converted files only Goober and Test.DRG changed.
     Between two keys each is split into scale, rotation and position (BaseLib's Float4x4Decompose), blended, the rotation by
     slerp, and put back.  What the split loses, the slight shear Goober's keys have, is blended straight, so each key is itself at
     its time and nothing jumps.
     Tracks hold Ptrs.  Sharing a walk between instances is step 8's.
     Tests prove it, not a picture: played at its start every bone of Goober's is where its file has it, and through the walk,
     at its keys and between them, no bone changes length.  RenderSceneFile --key poses it the same way, and draws it whole.
8. Skeletons, before skinning, so that skinning is written once against them.  Done: CBone and CSkeleton in SupportLib's
   Skeleton.h, the format's version 2, and every converted file made again.  A skeleton was spread over three places: the
   instance's connections and their parents, CMeshSkin's inverse matrices, and each CInstanceMesh's mapConnections.  A second
   Goober meant copying 67 connections and rewiring their parents and its mesh's list, and a walk only moved the Goober it was read
   with, as its tracks pointed at that one's connections.  The user's decisions:
     A CSkeleton is shared, and holds its CBones parents first.  A CBone is a CObject that doesn't change once built: its name, a
     Ptr to its parent bone and its rest local matrix.  The skeleton keeps each bone's index in it right as bones are added.
     Bones are known by what they are, not where they are.  A CConnection gains a Ptr<CBone>, NULL for what isn't a bone, and
     CMeshSkin holds the CBone each of its matrices is, so a bone added to a skeleton later breaks no clip or skin.
     A CSceneAnimation is one of two kinds.  A skeleton's holds a Ptr<CSkeleton>, each of its tracks a Ptr<CBone>, and it moves
     nothing until a player is bound to an instance with that skeleton: Goober's walk, shared by any number of Goobers.  The
     scene's own holds no skeleton, and each of its tracks a Ptr<CConnection>, as before: Melee's ships flying about, and
     Test.DRG's planets and the Point01 its camera hangs under.  A clip never mixes them, as a fixed connection in a shared clip
     would move the first instance's whatever instance played it.
     An instance made from a skeleton has a connection for each bone, in the skeleton's order, its local matrix the bone's rest one,
     and the root bone's parent is the instance's own connection.  A CAnimationPlayer bound to an instance finds each track's
     connection once, by its bone, and plays as it does now.  CInstanceMesh::mapConnections is bound the same way from the skin's
     bones, and becomes a cache: a file no longer holds it.  A walk on another skeleton needs a map from bone to bone (by name, or
     given), which waits until something needs it.
     What a skeleton holds is the exporter's and the converter's choice: the bones a skin uses, and the attachment points worth
     keeping.  For Goober that's its 67 bones and none of its 73 IK chain and finger helpers, whose effect is in the bones' keys.
     Its walk loses their tracks.
     The file has a Skeleton chunk listing the bones by index, which tracks and skins write, as references are indices into lists
     in the same file.  The reader makes them Ptrs and the writer indices again.  The version goes up, as the skin's matrices mean
     something new.  An Animation with a Skeleton chunk has tracks with a Bone, and one without has tracks with Attached.
   What doing it found:
     A connection that plays a bone says so with SkeletonBone (skeleton, bone), and an instance with a Skeleton gives a mesh posed
     by its bones no ConnectionSlots.  The writer checks at Close that every bone referred to is in its skeleton, and that such a
     mesh is only used by an instance with its skeleton.  A reader refuses version 1, whose skins were posed by their slots.
     Saving a scene reaches skeletons through its instances and animations, so a skin whose bones are in neither fails to save.
     The converter's helpers are narrower than "a connection only a track uses": only in an animation that moves bones is a track
     moving something else a helper, so the files with no skeleton came out the same but for their version, byte for byte.  Goober
     went from 141 connections and 140 tracks to 68 (goober_, which nothing uses, and the 67 bones) and 67.  A bone's parent is the
     nearest bone above it, skipping what isn't one, and a root bone hangs from the nearest kept connection above it; Goober's
     bone_goober_00 has none.  Its instance's connections are in the skeleton's order now, not the skin's.
     RenderSceneFile draws every converted file, posed at several keys, to the same bytes as before.  TestSceneGooberTwo makes a
     second Goober from the first's skeleton, ten along, sharing its mesh and walk at another time: the first is where it would be
     alone, no bone of either changes length, and at the same time they're ten apart.
9. Skinning, in software on the raster renderer.  Done.  BuildRasterMesh gives a skinned CRasterMesh's vertices their weights, and
   RasterLib's CRasterPosed poses them with integers only, from SRasterBones that RasterRenderLib's CRasterFrame makes each frame:
   each the skin's inverse matrix times the world matrix of the instance mesh's connection for it, placed as a rigid mesh is.  An
   SRasterInstance with pcPosed draws those vertices with the mesh's triangles, with no axes of its own.  The user's decisions:
     The posing is a pass of its own, into vertices of the instance's that the frame keeps from frame to frame (by the instance
     mesh's object index), and their bounds light it.  Not in the rasteriser's project jobs.
     A vertex has any number of weights, most first: integers of RASTER_SKIN_ONE (15 bits) adding up to exactly it, what rounding
     leaves over going to the largest.  Not at most four, which Vulkan may want.
     A normal is turned by each bone's rotation, blended, and made a unit again, by an integer square root.
     A bone the instance hasn't got: the mesh isn't drawn, and binding it logs that once (CInstanceMesh::BindBones).
     A bone whose matrix mirrors is an error: the mesh isn't drawn while it does, logged the first frame and not again while the
     instance mesh is drawn or tried.  That's a flag on the frame's record of it, as CLogger has no "once".  A CLogger::ErrorOnce
     taking such a flag was offered, and not added.
   Goober, loaded from its converted file and walked, poses at eight times to within 47 units of floats in 6 million: the axes'
   16 bits of fraction, as a rigid mesh has.  It draws in its material's colour, white, as RasterLib has no textures yet.  Its
   mesh is bound in another pose than its walk's first key, so posed at that key its edges are 0.09 to 4.4 times their length in
   the mesh, at its joints, though it draws right: an edge's length isn't a test of it.
10. In-place updates, if dragging plain meshes is slow.
11. The cook stage and VulkanLib.  The factory, and the build in RasterRenderLib, change from CMesh* to cooked data.  Done 2026-11-14.
   The user's decisions:
     The raster renderer needn't draw the same bytes once it's built from cooked data: cooking may split vertices at UV seams and
     colour a triangle by its submesh's material.
     The LunarG Vulkan SDK, on D:, for its headers, glslc and validation layers, and VulkanLib loads vulkan-1.dll itself, so that
     a built app runs with only the driver.  The Linux box needs libvulkan-dev, glslc and the validation layers.
     Vulkan draws offscreen for now, and the frame is read back into a CCanvas, so it works in any window or dialog and in tests.
     A swapchain on a native window is a step of its own later, with the WindowLib design it needs (see Open).
     Skinning is in the vertex shader, with each vertex's first weight and count, its weights and the palette in storage buffers,
     so a vertex keeps any number of weights.  What needs a posed mesh in main memory, physics say, poses it in the scene, in
     floats on demand (a SupportLib pose of an instance mesh, from its connections), not from the renderer: the same weights and
     inverse times world matrices, for the frame it's simulating, with or without a renderer.
     Vulkan lights as the rasteriser does (ambient, directional, point lights with a reach), so the two can be compared within a
     tolerance, and draws a material's diffuse texture.  Other textures wait.  Transparent triangles go after opaque, unsorted.
     It's proved by a test suite, TestVulkanLib, of offscreen frames compared with the raster renderer's.  A test scene in
     WindowInputTester comes later.
   The order: the cook stage with the raster renderer built from it; VulkanLib's device, offscreen target, a rigid mesh, camera
   and lights; textures; skinning (Goober walking); the SupportLib pose.
   What was done, and found:
     RenderLib's CCookedMesh has unique vertices, submeshes by material and transparency, and skin weights as floats, normalised
     and sorted most first.  CRenderScene cooks each editor's mesh, and both factories build from the cooked mesh.
     BaseLib's CSharedLibrary loads vulkan-1.dll (libvulkan.so.1 on Linux), and VulkanLib gets every function through it, with
     VK_NO_PROTOTYPES.  Vulkan 1.3, with dynamic rendering.  The validation layer is on in debug builds, its errors to gcLogger.
     A frame is drawn and read back synchronously: B8G8R8A8 colour, so a pixel is 0xAARRGGBB as a CCanvas has it, D32 depth, and
     host-visible buffers throughout.  Faster memory, and frames in flight, wait for the swapchain.
     A front face is counter-clockwise when it isn't mirrored and clockwise when it is: Vulkan's y points down the target, so the
     rasteriser's clockwise is wrong here.
     Textures are cached by their CImage until the renderer goes, linear, repeating, with no mipmaps; no texture is white 1x1.
     Skinning: each skinned mesh has a descriptor set of its skin, and the frame's palette (CInstanceMesh::GetPalette for each
     instance) is one buffer seen at a dynamic offset.  A skinned draw has 1 in its normal rows' first w; rigid draws bind an
     empty skin.  A mirroring bone is logged once and its mesh not drawn, as in CRasterFrame.
     CInstanceMesh::Pose gives positions only, in floats: normals are per face corner in a CMesh, and physics wants positions.
     TestVulkanLib: boxes lit and unlit within a mean 1.2 to 2.3 of 255 of the rasteriser, a texture's texels, skinned boxes, and
     Goober's walk covering the rasteriser's pixels to about 1 in 500.  It isn't in the test sweep yet, and Linux needs
     libvulkan-dev to build it with CMake (the headers compile in WSL).


Test scenes
-----------
BoardScene proves the rigid path, including the parent chain.  It has no textures, materials or skinning.

Goober, a skinned mesh walking a field of stars, proves the rest, and becomes a test scene in CommonLibraries once converted.  Its
source is D:\Work\Star Control\Builds\ViewerV7beta2009.9.20\Goober.DRG and Particles\Stars.csv beside it.  1 mesh (3241 positions,
6134 faces), 1 material, 3 images, 67 bones and 1105 stars.  The Max source is in D:\Work\Bounty Arms\MaxFiles\Goober.

What the converter and whatever poses it need to know:
  Pose it with vectors on the left of row-major matrices:
    fWeight * (position * inverse[iMatrix] * world matrix of the instance mesh's mapConnections[iMatrix])
  A connection's local matrix is its track's first key, so either poses it the same.  That a pose tore the mesh was the old files'
  numbering.
  Its old file's keys held world matrices, which the converter makes local.  Its file lost its parents, but the converter found
  them again: a connection's local matrix was still relative to its parent, and its parent came before it.  So its 67 bones are a
  skeleton 12 deep under bone_goober_00.  Where a bone and a finger helper are in the same place the first is taken, as either
  gives the same world matrix.  Its locals were the parent's matrix times the local one, the other way round to a scene file, so
  the converter works its locals out again from the world ones.
  Its stars are all in a box of size 1 with radii up to 0.0086.  The Viewer made them bigger somehow, which isn't in its file.
  A key's time is seconds from the start of the animation.  CAnimKeyFrame::mfTime is commented as time until the next frame, so
  don't copy one to the other.
  The walk is on the spot.  Moving across the stars is the instance's own connection.
  It has no stored normals.  They come from its smoothing groups.

Into Oblivion's link nodes have the path to them from their root, which is their parent, and their axis is their local matrix: its
game made a world matrix the parent's times the local one.  Only Boss has children: each turret is under its body and each rocket
under a turret.  Taking an axis as a world matrix had put the rockets inside the body.  Test.DRG's camera is under Point01.

Melee.DRG has 64 meshes with no faces, but AddOldDragonMaxFaces with D:\Work\Star Control\MaxFiles\Melee.max restores their faces,
UVs, names and materials (132,356 triangles), and OldDragonLib reads their colours.  Placement comes from the DRG's connections
and instances, as the .max's node transforms weren't decoded when last reported.  A textured material's colours are white in a
.DRG but not in the .max.


Open
----
Particles.  Goober's stars are neither a mesh nor an instance.  For now they're a list of CSceneParticles on a CScene, so that nothing in a file
is lost, and a point list has its own cook and draw on each renderer.

Modifiers that make other faces (weld, corner merge, edge collapse, fix reversed, merge normals) can't keep UVs, skin or materials,
as they only rebuild positions, normals and colours: CMeshModifier::CanRebuild refuses such a mesh and Modify returns it as it was.
Modifiers that only change normals, and shapes, keep everything since step 2.

Creating and removing an attached light.  Copying an instance doesn't bring its lantern along, so whatever creates the
character must also create the light and add it to the scene.  When the character is removed, something must remove the light,
or it stays lit where the hand was: its connection's parent keeps the hand's connection alive, but nothing updates it.

Coordinates.  A scene file is right handed with y up, but the KiCad boards look Z-up, so CMesh has no one convention.  Set one, and
importers convert to it.

Threads.  Cooking would run on CJobs and draws skip what isn't ready, but it isn't known yet that reading a CMesh off the main
thread is safe.

Vulkan in a window.  CCanvas is software pixels, while Vulkan presents to a surface made from the native window.  A Vulkan view in
a dialog needs a design of its own.
